Mouse eye keypoint tracking: what was done and what came out

Zhiheng Li (University of Minnesota), Kaiwen Sheng (Stanford) · page built 2026-11-07 15:42 · GitHub: ZhihengLi0/mouse-behavior-tracking

Task: DeepLabCut finds 8 points on every frame of a mouse-eye video: pupil top, bottom, left, right; upper and lower eyelid; nasal and temporal eye corner. From these we get pupil size, eye opening and blinks, to be aligned with brain recordings later.

Main question: On a new video, how many frames must a person label before the model is good enough?

The work has two phases, separated by the day the keypoint definitions were made explicit (2026-09-20). Phase 1 used the earlier definitions; only three choices that do not depend on the definitions are kept from it: batch size, model and error-correction algorithm. All other conclusions come from phase 2, which starts again from the same 5-minute video under the explicit definitions. Error numbers of the two phases cannot be compared directly.

Conclusions of phase 1 (steps 1–3)

Premise: pupil top is placed at the highest point of the visible pupil, the part not hidden by the eyelid.

  1. Which batch size? 2. Batches 2, 4, 8 and 16 cannot be told apart; 2 has the lowest validation loss on the 20 validation frames (step 1).
  2. Which model? ResNet-50 (step 2).
  3. Which error-correction algorithm picks the frames to label next? jump. The three algorithms show no clear gap; jump is slightly ahead (step 3).

The logic in between: why ResNet-50 and batch 2 were kept after the standard changed

Conclusion: ResNet-50 and batch 2, chosen in phase 1, are kept; they were not re-selected under the new definitions. Three steps of reasoning.

  1. The three comparisons of phase 1 were fair in themselves: all training and test frames of that video were labeled the same way at the time (checked in step 4), so the comparisons between batch sizes, between models and between error-correction algorithms hold.
  2. What changed on 2026-09-20 is where the points are placed (point-by-point comparison in step 4): pupil top moved from "the highest point of the visible pupil" to "the true top of the pupil ellipse", about 50 px higher; pupil left and right moved from "the leftmost and rightmost visible points of the pupil" to the two ends of the horizontal axis of the ellipse; the two corners moved from the inner side of the shadow and of the reflections to the outer side, 40–50 px outwards each. Pupil bottom and the two eyelid points did not change. The images, the task and the training procedure did not change.
  3. The choice of batch size and model depends on the images and the training procedure, not on where the points are placed, so both choices are unaffected.

This is a judgment, not an experimental result: batch size and model were not compared again under the explicit definitions.

Conclusions of phase 2 (steps 4–11)

Premise: the pupil is treated as an ellipse and pupil top is placed at the true top of that ellipse; where the eyelid hides it, the labeler estimates its position from the ellipse.

  1. How many labels are enough within one video? It saturates within about 80 labels: the same 5-minute video, relabeled under the keypoint definitions made explicit on 2026-09-20 and redone, gives 12.9 / 13.0 / 12.0 / 13.1 / 12.3 px at 20 / 40 / 60 / 80 / 100 labels, with no decrease; and no later video has a plateau point above 80 labels (step 5). In phase 1 the same video showed no decrease from 80 to 300 labels either (step 3).
  2. What about a new mouse? It needs its own labels; after 20 of them the error is down to about 10 px (step 5).
    • Second mouse (the model had seen one mouse): error 208 px with no labels, 10.4 px after 20.
    • Third mouse (the model had seen two mice): 26 px with no labels, 9.2 px after 20.
    • The more mice the model has seen, the smaller the starting error on a new mouse without labels; the 20 labels are still needed.
  3. How many labels does each later video of the same mouse need? Of the 16 later videos of the same mouse, 14 reach the plateau with 0–40 labels (0 labels: 2, 20: 6, 40: 6); video 14 needs 60 and video 9 needs 80 (step 5).
  4. 20 labels already come close to saturation; could the first batch be cut to 5 or 10? They cannot replace 20: 5 labels are enough on only 5 of the 19 videos and 10 labels on 7, one cannot tell in advance which, and the result depends on which frames happen to be taken. What is compared is the size of the first batch (5 or 10 taken out of the 20 already labeled), not a procedure of 5 labels per step (step 6).
  5. Do new videos make old ones worse? No (step 7).
  6. Which points are accurate, and can the confidence be trusted? Pupil top is the least accurate (median error 10.2 px; the other 7 points 3.5–6.0 px); for every point a lower confidence goes with a larger error, but pupil top has 8.3 px even at high confidence (step 8).
  7. Which frames of a whole video cannot be trusted, and how to repair them? A frame error above 14% of the eye width is a wrong frame, 23 of the 1330 truth frames; two finders, fitting for jumps in time with the same line as threshold and a PCA of the pose shape with a per-video threshold, find all 23; a thrown point is replaced by the second peak of its heatmap and accepted by the human-label range, 15 frames repaired, the rest stay flagged, nothing deleted; on the whole videos about six of seven repairs are right by eye Making finding, repair and check into a loop, with the checked or the repaired frames retraining the model next to the human labels, reduces the wrong frames on all four videos tried and brings the flagged share below a third; blind frames labeled afterwards confirm it; a third experiment that feeds both kinds back together and runs turn after turn also beats the starting model after its first turn on the three videos finished (step 9).
  8. How to compute the pupil area? Under the definitions made explicit on 2026-09-20 all four pupil points are ellipse endpoints, and on the 940 test frames of 19 videos the four-point area has a median error of 5.4% against 8.4% for the three-point rule; on videos 4 and 8 the area is still not accurate (step 10).
  9. How to detect blinks? Definition: not open = the eye is closed or the lid covers part of the pupil. The analysis here is static, frame by frame; blink dynamics are a separate analysis. Criterion: one pixel threshold per mouse, not open when the eyelid distance is below it, Pluto 147.5 px, Terra 150.5 px. The threshold is chosen on 327 judged frames (the labeler judged open / not open from the raw frame alone), 94% (Pluto) and 89% (Terra) accurate. The model's eyelid distance is within 2 px of the labels on 949 test frames. In 9 videos (3, 4, 8–12, 15, 18) 20–79% of the frames are below the threshold: a half-covered eye is the resting state of those recordings, and blinks are a subset (step 11).

Status: 19 videos finished (videos 0–18).

How the metrics are computed

Each video has a set of human-labeled test frames that are never used for training or for any choice. For every test frame the model gives the coordinates of the 8 points, in pixels of the original video frame (928 × 736, no rescaling). Every predicted point enters the calculation, none is dropped for low confidence; points the person did not label (for example the pupil points of a closed-eye frame) are left out.

  1. Error of one point: for point k of frame i, with prediction (x^ik,y^ik) and human label (xik,yik), the error is the straight-line distance between the two: eik=(x^ik−xik)2+(y^ik−yik)2
  2. Median frame RMSE (the main metric from step 3 on): first the root mean square of the point errors within each frame, RMSEi=1Ki∑keik2where Ki is the number of labeled points in that frame (at most 8); then the median of RMSEi over all test frames.
  3. RMSE over all keypoints (used in steps 1 and 2): one root mean square over the errors of all points of all test frames, RMSE=1N∑i,keik2where N is the total number of labeled points (100 frames × 8 points, at most 800). A few points that are far off pull it up, which is why the median frame RMSE was used later.
  4. The other numbers: "90th percentile" is the 90th percentile of RMSEi; "test frames above 50 px" is the share of frames with RMSEi > 50; "points with confidence ≥ 0.6" is the share of labeled points whose model confidence is at least 0.6.

Lower is better. For scale: under the present keypoint definitions the eye width (distance between the two corners, median of the human labels on each video's test frames) is 363–497 px, so 10 px is about 2–3% of the eye width and 50 px about 10–14%. The scoring scripts are in the repository: v1_old_pupil_top/batch-size-selection/scripts/evaluate_eye_test_set.py and the script of the same name under model-selection/scripts/ for steps 1 and 2, active-learning-jump-selection/scripts/round_train_pipeline.py for step 3, and v2_new_pupil_top_video_scaling/scripts/scale_step.py from step 5 on; every score is written to the csv tables of the results/ folders. The video, data, model and metric of every step are listed in the "Basic information" table at its start.

Training recipe (chosen in steps 1 and 2, used in both phases): ResNet-50, batch 2. Every model is trained anew from ImageNet-pretrained weights; it never continues from one of our earlier models.

Basic information

VideoThe 5-minute eye video of the first mouse (60 fps, 18,000 frames). The first 4 minutes are used for frame selection and training, the final minute for testing only.
Label standardPhase 1.
Training dataStarts from the 80 frames of step 1. Each round the model predicts all 14,400 frames of the first 4 minutes, the detector flags suspicious frames, 20 of them are picked by clustering image appearance, all 8 points are corrected by hand and the frames are added to the training set. The three detectors accumulate independently; after 11 rounds each branch has 300 frames.
Validation dataThe 20 frames of step 1, never changed.
Test data100 frames evenly spaced in time over the final minute, all 8 points labeled by hand. Used only to report the score, never for training or for any choice.
Model and settingsResNet-50, batch 2, 100 epochs, learning rate drops at epochs 80 and 95, trained on the Apple GPU (MPS). Every round is trained anew from ImageNet-pretrained weights. Each point in the figure is the snapshot with the best internal validation mAP, from a single run.
MetricMedian frame RMSE: the RMSE over the 8 points of each frame, then the median over the 100 frames.

What was done:Choose an error-correction algorithm. Once a model is trained, an algorithm searches the video for frames where the model is probably wrong, a person corrects only those frames and the model is retrained; this is active learning. Three algorithms were compared to see whose picks reduce the error most.

Procedure. Start from 80 labels. Each round: the algorithm flags suspect frames in the prediction of the whole video → the flagged frames are clustered into 20 groups by image appearance, one frame per group → a person corrects these 20 frames and they are added to the training set → retrain. 11 rounds, from 80 to 300 labels. Each algorithm runs as its own branch.

The three algorithms (all built into DeepLabCut, extract_outlier_frames; input = the model's prediction of every frame of the video):

  • uncertain: frames in which any of the 8 points has confidence below 0.6.
  • jump: frames in which any point moved more than 20 px from the previous frame.
  • fitting: frames in which the 8 points deviate on average by more than 20 px from a time-series model (ARIMA, fitted on the confident frames only) of each point's trajectory.

Conclusion:This step has two conclusions: jump is chosen as the error-correction algorithm, and on this video about 80 labels saturate.

  1. jump is the error-correction algorithm and picks the frames in all later experiments. In this experiment jump has the lowest error: the mean of the median error over the 11 rounds is 16.4 px for jump, 17.0 px for uncertain and 17.0 px for fitting, and jump is the lowest in 8 of the 11 rounds. The lead is about 0.6 px, smaller than the variation when one setting is trained again (0.3–2 px), so we regard the three error-correction algorithms as close, with jump slightly ahead.
  2. On the jump threshold: this step used a fixed 20 px. From step 5 on it is 3% of the video's median eye width; the algorithm is unchanged, and videos with different eye widths share one relative standard (eye widths 363–497 px, threshold about 11–15 px). Under the present corner definition this 5-minute video's eye is 497 px wide, so 20 px is about 4%: the step-5 threshold is slightly stricter than this one.
  3. On this video about 80 labels saturate. From 80 to 300 labels the median error of all three branches stays between 15.7 and 17.5 px, with no decrease.
    • This result uses the keypoint definitions from before 2026-09-20.
    • After the definitions were made explicit on 2026-09-20, the same video was relabeled and the experiment repeated, with the same conclusion: the error stays between 12 and 13 px at 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 labels and does not fall with the number of labels (step 5, video 0). What was made explicit is described in step 4; mainly, pupil top moved from "the highest point of the visible pupil" to "the true top of the pupil ellipse", and the two corners became the crossing of the continued eyelid margins.
    • The error values under the two sets of definitions cannot be compared directly; what agrees is that the video saturates within about 80 labels.

4Keypoint definitions made explicit (from 2026-09-20): pupil as an ellipse, corners at the crossing of the eyelid margins

What was done:Where each of the 8 points is placed was written down, and all earlier videos were relabeled accordingly. From this step on every model and number uses these definitions. The full definitions, example figures and the common mistakes are in the folder keypoint_definitions/ of the repository. The points are described one group at a time below, each followed by its figure.

  • Pupil top, bottom, left, right (Fig. 4-2): the pupil is treated as an ellipse and the four points are the ends of its two axes. Parts hidden by the upper eyelid or by fur are completed with the ellipse, so the pupil-top point often lies above the upper-eyelid point, that is, at a position covered by the lid. In Fig. 4-2 the red dots are the present positions; the yellow crosses are the positions before 2026-09-20: at the topmost, bottommost, leftmost and rightmost visible points of the pupil, without fitting an ellipse. The pupil boundary is the outer edge of the darkened region; where the edge looks doubled the left contour is used; the brighter band around the dark core counts as pupil.
  • Upper and lower eyelid (Fig. 4-1 above): the line between the eyelid skin and the eyeball is the eyelid margin. The upper-eyelid point is the midpoint of the upper margin, the lower-eyelid point the midpoint of the lower margin.
  • Left corner (nasal, medial canthus; Fig. 4-3, second row): there is a shadow at this corner; it gets narrower downwards and continues to near the lower eyelid. The outer border of the shadow is taken as the lower-left margin (solid red); it and the upper margin are each continued (dashed red), and the crossing of the two continuations (red circle) is the left corner; the cyan dot is the labeled position. Not used (dotted yellow line and yellow cross): following the inner border of the shadow, which puts the crossing inside the shadow. The hatched area is the shadow between the two constructions.
  • Right corner (temporal, lateral canthus; Fig. 4-3, third row): there are several bright reflections on this side, with a stretch of shadow below them. The lower margin is continued upwards along the outer line (dashed red), around the right of the reflections, and the point where it meets the upper margin (red circle) is the right corner; the cyan dot is the labeled position. Why the outer side: fur does not reflect, so the bright reflections on the right are still inside the eye and the corner must lie outside them. Not used (dotted yellow line and yellow cross): following the inner edge of the reflection and of the shadow below it, which puts the crossing on the inner line next to the reflection. The corner is not placed on a reflection either.
  • Points that cannot be seen: the same for all 8 points: a point is labeled when it can be seen clearly and left empty when its position cannot be judged accurately.

Validation data20 frames from the part between 80% and 90% of the video, with 2-second guards on both sides.
Test data50 frames evenly spaced in time over the final 10% of the video, labeled by hand and frozen. Used only to report the score.
Model and settingsResNet-50, batch 2, 120 epochs, learning rate drops at epochs 96 and 114, trained on MPS, scored on CPU. Every step is trained anew from ImageNet-pretrained weights, a single run. What was done:Videos are added one at a time, in this order.

  • Video 0: the 5-minute video of the first mouse.
  • Videos 1, 2, 3: the second mouse, Pluto; three recordings of the same day (2025-10-31), 19.9 minutes each.
  • Videos 4–10: Pluto, one recording per recording day, from the most recent backwards: 10-30, 10-29, 10-28, 10-27, 10-24, 10-23, 10-22 (there is no video of 10-26 or 10-25 in our set).
  • From video 11: the third mouse, Terra, one recording per recording day, from the most recent backwards: video 11 is 2025-10-17, video 12 is 10-16, then 10-15, 10-14, 10-13, 10-10, 10-09, 10-08 (videos 13–18).

Every video follows the same steps:

  1. 50 frames evenly spaced over the final 10% of the video become the test set; they are labeled and frozen.
  2. The previous model is applied unchanged to get the "0 labels" error.
  3. Each step adds 20 labels of this video (picked by the algorithm of section 5.1 and Figure 5-1 below; the model pre-labels each batch and a person corrects every frame), retrains together with the labels carried from earlier videos, and scores on the test set.
  4. Plateau rule: stop when two consecutive steps each fail to improve the best error by more than 3%. The plateau point is the last step that did improve it. When the rule fires, a person confirms whether the video is finished. All 19 videos stopped when the rule fired, so all of them meet the definition of the plateau; for 18 of them the plateau point is the step the rule gives, and only for video 5 was the plateau point given further consideration. On video 5 the median error at 20 labels (15.85 px) and at 40 labels (16.58 px) is no better than at 0 labels (11.90 px), so the rule fired at 40; this meets the definition of the plateau, and by the rule the plateau point is 0 labels. But we took into account that the 0-label model is off by more than 50 px on 6 of the 50 test frames (12%), which are gross errors; with 20 labels there are 0 such frames, so all gross errors are removed; with 40 labels there are still 0, so the effect holds and nothing further is gained. The plateau point is therefore taken as 20 labels, and these 20 labels are what later videos carry.
  5. The next video starts, carrying only this video's labels up to the plateau point (not every frame labeled on this video goes into the training of later videos: frames labeled after the plateau point are left out). This mirrors real use, where labeling stops at the plateau.

5.1 How each batch of 20 frames is picked (algorithm)

Script: v2_new_pupil_top_video_scaling/scripts/select_frames.py. All numbers below are fixed parameters of the script; following them gives the same frames.

(a) The video is first split into three segments

  1. Test segment: the final 10% of the video (at least 60 s). 50 frames evenly spaced in time are the test frames.
  2. Validation segment: the 10% before it (at least 60 s). 20 evenly spaced frames are the validation frames.
  3. Pool: all frames before the validation segment (about the first 80%; 57158 frames for a 19.9-min video). Training frames are picked from the pool only. 2 s are removed on both sides of the validation segment so that the three segments share no near-identical neighbouring frames.

(b) Batch 1: clustering by image appearance (no model)

  1. Every 5th frame of the pool is taken.
  2. An "appearance fingerprint" is computed for each frame: greyscale, downscaled to 128 × 96; a Gaussian blur with sigma 1 px minus a Gaussian blur with sigma 6 px (keeps edges and shape, removes slow lighting changes); downscaled to 32 × 24 and flattened to 768 numbers; mean subtracted and divided by the standard deviation.
  3. k-means on the fingerprints with 20 clusters (scikit-learn KMeans, n_clusters=20, n_init=10, max_iter=300, random_state=42).
  4. From each cluster the frame closest to the cluster centre (Euclidean distance) is taken, 20 frames in total, starting with the largest cluster.
  5. Spacing: picked frames must be at least 1 s apart from each other and from every frame labeled earlier; frames that violate this are skipped. If fewer than 20 remain, the frames already taken are kept, the number of clusters is raised to 30, 40, … (at most 80) and re-clustered, and the new centre frames are taken in order of cluster size, those that satisfy the spacing, only until 20 are reached.

(c) From batch 2 on: candidates by the jump rule, then clustering by appearance

  1. The model of the previous step predicts every frame of the whole video.
  2. Threshold (one pixel number per video): eye width = median over the video of the distance between the two corners in each frame; threshold = 3% of it, about 11–15 px. The eye width only sets this number and plays no part in the selection.
  3. Displacement of each frame: how many pixels each of the 8 points moved from the previous frame; the largest of the 8.
  4. The rule: largest displacement of the 8 points > eye width × 3% → the frame is a candidate (a video's eye width is one fixed number, so the threshold is constant over the video; any one point jumping far counts). With more than 12000 candidates only about 12000 are kept, evenly spaced (to bound the clustering cost); with fewer than 40, the 2000 frames with the largest displacement are used instead.
  5. The candidates go through exactly the steps of batch 1: fingerprints, k-means with 20 clusters, the frame closest to each centre, at least 1 s apart; if fewer than 20, the number of clusters is raised to 30, 40, … and re-clustered, topping up only the shortfall in order of cluster size.
  6. If there are still fewer than 20 (this happens when the model is already good and the few candidates are bunched within a few seconds), the batch is filled from the candidates in order of decreasing jump, still 1 s apart.

Every batch is therefore "one frame per cluster": the jump rule decides where to look (frames on which the model is unstable), and the clustering makes sure the 20 picked frames look different from each other instead of being 20 nearly identical frames.

9.1 Defining the wrong-frame threshold: error in units of eye width, the line at 14% of the eye width

Errors are in units of eye width rather than pixels so that the same numbers hold on another camera or at another distance. The histogram of the 1330 errors (Figure 9-1) has one main group that ends at about 13% of the eye width and, to its right, a sparse tail. The line is at 14% of the eye width; there is no frame between 12.7% and 15.4%. Frames above the line are wrong frames, every other frame counts as right. The line was read off the histogram, as the blink threshold of step 11 was.

Why the gap between the two groups is empty.

  1. Below the line: the model's points are still on the structure they belong to and the deviation is only a slide along it: for a pupil point which ring of the edge it sits on, for a corner or lid point a shift along the lid margin, so it cannot exceed the size of that structure. Taking the pupil points as the example, in the 1307 frames of the main group the model's worst pupil point is at a median 1.16 pupil radii from the pupil centre, nine in ten within 1.4.
  2. Above the line: the heatmap's highest peak has jumped to another structure: a pupil point to a corner, an eyelid, a glint or the image border, a corner or lid point to the fur, the image border or the other corner. Of the 23 wrong frames, 13 have only pupil points thrown, 3 only points of the eye outline, 7 both; taking the pupil points as the example, the worst point is at a median 5 radii from the pupil centre.
  3. Nothing in between: the model scores high only where the image looks like the point it has learned. The pupil edge, the corners, the lid margins and the glints each have their own look, and peaks appear only there. Between the right place and the next structure lies a uniform stretch of iris, skin or fur in which no point was ever placed, so the model gives it no high score and no peak forms there. A point therefore either stays where it belongs or jumps to another structure far away; it does not stop just off its place. This holds for the pupil points and for the corner and lid points alike, and the heatmap examples of step 9.3 (figure 9-7) show it: of the two peaks one sits at the right place and one on the fur or the frame border, with nothing between them.
Conclusion on wrong frames:one line, 14% of the eye width.
  1. 23 of the 1330 frames are above it (videos 1: 8, 2: 4, 4: 2, 8: 1, 10: 3, 11: 4, 14: 1); on these videos the line is about 55–69 px.
  2. Below the line the points are still on their own structure, above it a point has jumped to another structure; the gap between the two makes the exact position of the line uncritical.
  3. Every threshold that follows uses the same unit and the same line.

9.2 Settling the method for finding wrong frames: fitting sees jumps in time, the PCA sees implausible shapes, the two complement each other

A finder looks only at the model's predictions, gives every frame one number and flags the frame when the number crosses a threshold. The candidates were compared at the 14% line (Figure 9-3); this page uses two: fitting sees jumps in time, the PCA sees implausible shapes. Both find all 23 wrong frames, but not the same frames.

  1. jump: the displacement of the point that moved farthest since the previous frame, divided by the eye width.
  2. uncertain: the lowest confidence of the 8 points.
  3. fitting: a time-series model (SARIMAX, one fit per whole video, points with confidence below 0.01 treated as missing) fitted to each point's x and y trajectory predicts the point's position in this frame from the preceding frames; a point's deviation = distance between predicted and actual position, divided by the eye width. DeepLabCut's default takes the mean deviation of the 8 points, order (3,0,1); this page takes the largest of the 8, order (2,0,0).
  4. PCA: the human-labeled poses the video's model was trained on (8 points, 16 coordinates, the 17–73 frames up to the plateau) go through a principal component analysis keeping 99% of the variance, which gives the subspace of "normal poses"; the predicted pose of every frame is projected onto it and reconstructed, and the RMS of the 8 points' reconstruction residuals divided by the eye width is the frame's score; a single point's residual says which point to repair. It does not look at neighbouring frames, so a single frame suffices, but it needs the video's own training labels.
  5. Threshold of fitting = the line, 14% of the eye width.
    • The line of the histogram in 9.1 (Figure 9-1): there a frame whose error exceeds 14% of the eye width is a wrong frame, here a frame whose farthest point deviates by more than 14% of the eye width is flagged; the same number with the same meaning.
  6. Threshold of the PCA = 3.9 × the video's median. The rule has two numbers:
    • The median: computed per video, the median PCA score of all its frames. Most frames are normal, so it is the video's own background; it comes from the predictions, needs no labels, and is there for any new video. A fixed share of the eye width, as for fitting, will not do, because the size of the PCA score depends on how many training labels it saw: a fixed share flags most of a video when labels are few and still 17% of the worst video when they are many; as a multiple of the median the share flagged no longer depends on the number of labels and the worst video is at 9% (Figure 9-4).
    • 3.9: one number shared by all videos, set once on the truth frames. Dividing each of the 23 wrong frames' scores by its video's median, the smallest is 7.2, so a threshold at 7.2 × the median just encloses all 23; widened by the same factor by which the fitting line at 14% sits below its nearest wrong frame at 25.7%, 1.84, that gives 7.2 / 1.84 = 3.9. Set on 18 videos and tested on the held-out 19th, it finds all of them in every round.

Check by eye: when the two signals disagree, which is right? On the whole videos the two signals flag different frames. The frames one flags and the other does not have no labels, so only the eye can say whether they are real errors or false alarms.

Any of the 8 points can be a candidate.
  2. Second peak. A candidate is replaced by the second peak of its heatmap: the peak's cell times the stride of 8 px, plus the point's median offset between regressed coordinate and first-peak cell over the whole video. Second peak = among the local maxima of the heatmap at least 3 cells from the highest cell, the one with the highest score.
  3. Check, pupil part. The frame's own eye frame is built from the corners N (nasal) and T (temporal): u = T − N, Wf = ‖u‖, e = u/Wf, n = e rotated 90° counter-clockwise, origin C = (N + T)/2; the eye coordinates of any point p are(a,b)=((p−C)·eWf,(p−C)·nWf)With the four pupil points (atop, btop), (abot, bbot), (aleft, bleft), (aright, bright), the eyelid points' coordinates along n blid,top and blid,bot, pupil height h = |bbot − btop|, pupil width w = |aright − aleft|, the midline of top and bottom mtb = (atop + abot)/2 and of left and right mlr = (bleft + bright)/2, each pupil point k gives three quantities, 12 in all: its position along the corners ak; its position inside the eyelid bandlk=bk−blid,topblid,bot−blid,topand its ratio to the other three pointsρleft=smtb−alefth,ρright=saright−mtbh,ρtop=s′mlr−btopw,ρbot=s′bbot−mlrwwhere s, s′ are the video's left-right and top-bottom signs (the signs of the medians of aright − aleft and bbot − btop over the whole-video prediction), so that the ratios are positive on normal frames. Each quantity's range = minimum to maximum over the 1330 human labels, widened on each side by 0.42 of its width; 0.42 is the smallest margin at which the range of any 18 videos contains all labels of the 19th (ranges in label_region_margin.csv, for example aleft −0.65 to 0.20, ρtop 0.02 to 1.00). The part passes when all 12 are inside.
  4. Check, outline part. References are medians of the plateau model's whole-video prediction (no labels): median eye width Wm, median angle of the corner line θm, median corner midpoint Cm, with u = T − N. Three corner quantities:WfWm,atan2(uy,ux)−θm,‖(N+T)/2−Cm‖WmFive eyelid quantities in the frame's eye frame: the components of the upper eyelid point Et and the lower eyelid point Eb along e and along n (4 quantities), and the opening = the difference of the two eyelid points' components along n. Ranges again from the minimum and maximum over the 1330 labels, each part with its own margin by the same construction, 0.23 for the outline (for example eye-width ratio 0.56 to 1.22, angle deviation −8.3° to 10.2°, opening 0.013 to 0.76; values in label_region_outline.csv). All 8 are scale-free and carry over to another camera.
  5. The frame as it is first. A frame is first checked unchanged; if both parts pass it is judged plausible and left alone; only otherwise are points replaced. A replaced corner moves the eye frame, so the 12 pupil quantities are recomputed and re-checked in the new frame.
  6. Smallest subset. With the candidate set S, its subsets are tried in order: single outline points, pairs of outline points, single pupil points, pairs containing a pupil point, and so on; each subset puts in the second peak of its points and keeps the rest as they are; the first subset that passes both parts is the repair; if none passes, the frame is reverted and stays flagged.
  7. Each frame's status is one of: not flagged, plausible as it is (flagged, check passed, untouched), repaired, flagged and not repaired, closed. Whole videos are counted in these five classes; on the truth frames the error before and after says repaired to good or made worse.

Why the second peak. For each point the model outputs not a coordinate but a heatmap: the image in cells of 8 px, one score per cell for how likely the point is there; the reported coordinate is the highest cell plus an offset inside the cell that the network outputs as well, so it is precise to the pixel rather than to 8 px, and the reported confidence is that highest score. A thrown point is a frame in which the highest cell landed elsewhere, on a corner, the fur or the image border, while the place the point belongs usually still holds a local high cell: the second peak. The 3 cells are an exclusion radius we set, so that a cell on the slope of the highest peak is not taken as the second peak. The second peak is known only as a cell, the network's offset holds only for the highest cell, so a repaired coordinate is precise to the cell, within half a cell, which does not matter for a right-or-wrong decision at the scale of tens of px. Figure 9-7 shows three frames the check by eye judged repaired correctly: the two peaks score almost the same, 0.27 against 0.25, 0.21 against 0.21, 0.42 against 0.38, so the model cannot tell which one is right and the confidence cannot replace the check.

9.4 Closing the loop: the frames that passed the check and the repaired frames retrain the model, and the model gets better

The question: after 9.2 finds the wrong frames and 9.3 repairs part of them, the frames that passed the check and the repaired frames have trustworthy coordinates nobody has looked at. The four pupil points are top T, bottom B, left L, right R, with coordinates (x, y) in pixels; the image y axis points down.

  1. Truth (human labels): the four points are the endpoints of the two axes of an ellipse, so the area isA=π4·w·h,w=|xR−xL|,h=|yB−yT|
  2. Four-point rule (model): the same formula on the model's four points.
  3. Three-point rule (top unused): the width is unchanged and the height is twice the distance from the bottom point to the midline of the left and right points,Ano top=π4·w·2(yB−yL+yR2)The rules without the bottom, left or right point are built the same way (twice the distance from the opposite point to the midpoint of the other axis).
  4. The other rules: "drop lowest confidence": per frame, leave out the pupil point with the lowest model confidence; "four, fallback": the four-point rule when all four confidences are at least 0.6, otherwise drop the lowest; "four + median filter": first the four-point area of every frame of the whole video, A1, A2, …; with a window of k frames (5 or 31; 31 frames = 0.5 s at 60 frames/s) the filtered area of frame i is the median of the k frames centred on it: Ãi = median(Ai−(k−1)/2, …, Ai+(k−1)/2); at the start and end of the video, where fewer than k frames exist, the median of the frames that do exist (pandas rolling(k, center=True, min_periods=1).median()). A median only replaces a frame by the middle value of its neighbours, so it corrects isolated wrong frames and cannot correct a run of wrong frames longer than (k−1)/2.
  5. Area error of a frame (%):ei=100×|A^i−Ai|Aiwhere A^i is the area on the model side and Ai the truth. Per video and for the 19 videos pooled, the median and the 90th percentile of ei are reported.
Conclusion on the area:Among these rules the four-point rule has the lowest error, but on some videos the area is still not accurate.
  1. Pooled over the 19 videos the four-point rule has a median error of 5.4%, the lowest group of the 9 rules; the three-point rule 8.4%; leaving out the bottom, left or right point 9.7–11.0%.
  2. Per video the four-point rule is lower than the three-point rule on 15 of the 19 videos; on videos 1, 2, 5 and 7 the three-point rule is lower.
  3. A median filter over time does not help (5 frames 5.2%, 31 frames 5.3%): the error is mostly not frame-to-frame jitter but runs of consecutive frames that are off together, which a median cannot correct.
  4. The error differs a lot between videos: 1.5–10% on 13 videos, 10.5–13.3% on 4 (videos 5, 10, 15, 18), 43.8% on video 4 and 24.0% on video 8, video 8 is also the video with the largest keypoint error (step 5: 25.34 px) and video 4 the third (15.01 px; video 5 is at 15.85 px but its area error is only 10.7%). The pooled 90th percentile is 34%: in one frame of ten the area is off by about a third or more.
  5. Why the four-point rule is better: both rules use the same width; they differ in the height. The four-point rule measures the height directly from top to bottom. The three-point rule has no top point, so it assumes that the left and right points lie exactly at mid-height of the ellipse and takes twice the distance from the bottom point to the line through them. A labeler cannot place the left and right points exactly at mid-height; they are always a few pixels too high or too low. The four-point rule uses only the x coordinates of these two points and is not affected, whereas the three-point rule doubles this offset into the height. Applying the three-point formula to the human points themselves (no model involved) differs from the human four-point area by a median of 5.0% over the 940 frames (13.0% on video 3, 9.0% on video 6). The model learns from the human labels and carries the same offset.

How the threshold is chosen (Figure 11-1). Each mouse uses only its own judged frames. Candidate T runs from 60 to 300 px in steps of 1 px.

Each T splits the frames into four cells:

  • hit: truth not open, O < T
  • miss: truth not open, O ≥ T
  • false alarm: truth open, O < T
  • correct rejection: truth open, O ≥ T

Three rates:

  • recall = hits ÷ frames whose truth is not open
  • false-alarm rate = false alarms ÷ frames whose truth is open
  • agreement (accuracy) = (hits + correct rejections) ÷ all frames

Choice: the orange line is recall − false-alarm rate, the blue line the agreement. The T with the highest orange value is taken, the middle of a tied range.

Why orange rather than blue: the agreement pools the two classes and leans toward whichever is more frequent. In the extreme, with 90% of the frames open, a threshold of 0 that flags nothing still agrees 90%. Recall − false-alarm rate takes one rate per class and subtracts, so it does not depend on the class proportions. Here the classes are nearly balanced (Pluto 77 against 74, Terra 64 against 62) and both lines peak at the same T.

With the actual numbers:

  • Terra, 126 frames (64 not open, 62 open). T = 150.5: hits 59, misses 5, false alarms 9, correct rejections 53. Recall 59 ÷ 64 = 92.2%, false-alarm rate 9 ÷ 62 = 14.5%, orange 92.2 − 14.5 = 77.7, agreement (59 + 53) ÷ 126 = 88.9%.
  • Pluto, 151 frames (77 not open, 74 open). T = 147.5: hits 71, misses 6, false alarms 3, correct rejections 71. Recall 71 ÷ 77 = 92.2%, false-alarm rate 3 ÷ 74 = 4.1%, orange 92.2 − 4.1 = 88.2, agreement (71 + 71) ÷ 151 = 94.0%.

Conclusion:One pixel threshold per mouse, Pluto 147.5 px, Terra 150.5 px, almost the same for the two mice.
  • Basis: on the mouse's judged frames, the threshold with the highest recall − false-alarm rate (Figure 11-1). Accuracy Pluto 94.0%, Terra 88.9%.
  • Stability: leave-one-video-out ranges Pluto 147.5–160.5 px, Terra 143.5–154.5 px. Pluto's optimum is flat (92–94% accuracy from 147 to 160 px); Terra's is sharp because many frames of videos 11 and 18 have their opening right at the threshold.
  • Two checks: the judged frames separate on the distribution of all frames, the misjudged ones almost all within 20 px of the threshold (Figure 11-3); the model's eyelid distance is within a median 2 px of the labels and moves only 2–4% of the test frames across the threshold (Figure 11-4). So most errors near the threshold come from borderline frames being hard to judge, not from the model.
  • Day by day: videos 0, 5, 6, 7, 13, 16, 17 have at most 1.1% of their frames below the threshold; videos 1, 2, 14 have 3–14%; videos 3, 4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15, 18 have 20–79%, among them videos 9, 10, 11, 18 with 59–79%, a narrowed eye all day. Not-open is a sustained state in these recordings; blinks are only a subset of it.
  • Limits: one judge, repeatability not measured. The sample is stratified by opening, so the accuracies are those of the stratified sample, not per-frame accuracies of whole videos. The threshold is scored on the frames it was chosen on; leave-one-video-out is the only check of that. On Pluto's three earliest days the eye width is 3–12% smaller; the same threshold is used (see the premise check). All 17 judged frames of video 18 are not open, so Terra's threshold is driven mainly by the days narrowed all day. Mouse A cannot be set. 330 frames drawn, 3 not judged.

13Where there is no conclusion yet

  • Unreliable frames: finding and repairing wrong frames now has one pipeline (9.2, 9.3): two finders, fitting for jumps in time and the PCA for implausible shapes; all 23 wrong frames are found and 15 repaired.
